Why Safeguard Rejects Work Orders (and How to Document Yours So It Does Not Happen)

Feuern Outsourcing · Documentation, Bids & Reopen Prevention · Updated October 2026

Not every work order gets reopened because the work was done poorly. Sometimes, it gets reopened because the paperwork didn't properly prove the work was completed. After processing thousands of preservation work orders across Safeguard, MCS, Five Brothers, Cyprexx, and other nationals, I've seen the same documentation gaps come up over and over. Here's the complete standard we follow, the photos we take for every order, and the money those photos help protect.

1 The one rule behind every rejection

Make the reviewer able to approve without asking anything

Every rejection we see traces back to the same thing. The reviewer got a work order that left one question open, and the fastest way to close a question is to send the order back. Not because the crew did anything wrong, but because nothing in the submission answered it.

So the standard is simple, even if it takes discipline: a reviewer who has never been to the property should be able to look at your submission and approve it without contacting anybody. That means the angles, the measurements, the dates, the sequence and the wording all have to line up. When they do, work gets approved on the first pass and paid on time.

The four things a reviewer is checking, every single time: was this the right property, was this the right work, was the work actually done, and is the amount correct. Every photo and every sentence you submit should answer one of those four.

2 The photos every work order needs

The compulsory set, no matter what the work order says

Work order instructions differ. What they all need does not. We take this set on every order, even when the instruction only asks for one thing, because the instruction is the minimum, not the whole job.

Property and address

  • Street sign and mailbox, so the property is identified beyond doubt
  • House number clearly readable
  • Full front elevation
  • Left side, right side and rear elevations
  • Driveway and walkway
  • Yard and lot, wide shots
  • Exterior A/C unit
  • Any outbuildings, sheds, detached garage, fence line and gate

Mechanical and utility

  • Hot water heater, whole unit, with the bottom visible
  • Washer hookups, capped or not
  • Furnace or air handler, so the heating type is on record
  • Electrical service panel, with the main breaker position visible
  • Volt stick or tester at an outlet for power verification
  • Water meter, gas meter and shut offs where relevant

Sign in when you enter

If you are entering the property, you sign in. If a sign in sheet is already posted on the kitchen cabinet, sign on that one. If there is not one, bring your own, sign it, and photograph it in place before you start working. It is the simplest document in the file and the one an auditor asks for first.

3 Securing and Attention stickers

The evidence that the property was properly secured and someone was notified

When you secure a property, the sticker on the door is part of the work. It tells the occupant, a code officer or a realtor who to call, and it tells the reviewer when the property was secured and by which company. A lockout with no sticker photo is a lockout the client cannot prove happened on the date you say.

Before you leave, check the front door and the visible windows. If a Safeguard securing sticker is not present, put one up, then photograph it straight on, readable, with the date stamp on. The same goes for the Attention notice that goes with a winterization or a secure, the one that tells anyone entering which breakers to leave off, that the water is off and who to call if the property is not actually abandoned.

What the stickers actually say, and why it matters

The Important notice states that the property was found vacant or abandoned, that the mortgage holder has the right to protect it, that it may be rekeyed or winterized within 3 days, and that if the property is not vacant the occupant must contact Safeguard Properties at 877-340-8482. It also lists the code enforcement contact for building officials. Get the whole notice in one readable frame, plus a close up of the date line.

The Attention notice is the one that protects the next person through the door. Breakers left in position, do not turn on water without a walk through inspection, the property may have been winterized, do not connect electricity or gas. Photograph it where it is posted, not on a table.

4 Proving utilities and heat

Power on, water off, heat type on record

Utility questions cause a surprising share of reopens, because they are the easiest thing to get wrong and the hardest to prove after the fact. Three frames settle almost all of it:

  • Power: volt stick or tester at a live outlet, light visible, outlet visible, date stamp on.
  • Water: the main valve position, and the water heater and washer hookups capped where the order requires winterization.
  • Heat: the furnace or air handler data plate, so the heating system type is documented and nobody has to guess later.

If you capped something, photograph the cap installed, then a wider frame showing where it is. Close up for the detail, wide shot for the location. Together they answer both "was it done" and "where".

5 The full interior walkthrough

On initial and inspection orders, nothing goes unphotographed

On an initial secure, an initial grass cut, a convey review or any order where the client is seeing the property for the first time, the photos are the property record. Anything you did not photograph is a bid you cannot submit and a condition you cannot prove existed before you arrived. That protects the client, and it protects you from being blamed for damage you did not cause.

Every room

  • Floor, walls and ceiling, wide then close
  • Baseboards, door frames, window frames and sills
  • Any crack, stain, hole, water damage or soft spot
  • Windows, inside and out, broken glass, missing screens, damaged locks
  • Doors, including closet and interior doors

Kitchen and laundry

  • All appliances, including inside the refrigerator for perishable debris
  • Under sink, for leaks and cabinet damage
  • Laundry room, washer hookups, caps, dryer vent
  • Hot water heater, full unit and bottom

Bathrooms

  • Each toilet, tank and bowl condition
  • Tub, shower, surrounds and caulking
  • Vanity, sink and supply lines
  • Exhaust fan and any ceiling staining

Structure and site

  • Garage, inside and out, attached or detached
  • Attic, if there is access, roof structure and insulation
  • Basement or crawl space
  • Roof and gutters where accessible. Walking the roof and photographing the gutters and downspouts
  • Any tree, shrub or vine touching or damaging the structure or fence
  • Property boundary, fences, gates, damage and debris

Think like the bid you are about to write. Every condition you photograph on an initial is a bid you can submit with proof. Every condition you walk past is money the client will not pay for, and a problem that will be blamed on the next crew. The initial walkthrough is where the whole job is set up.

6 Order types, and what each one wants

Same property, very different expectations

The industry runs on a handful of order types, and they group into three families. Knowing which family you are in tells you how much documentation the client expects before you submit anything.

Initial type Bid request Approval type

Initial type

Lock and grass cut, initial secure, convey review. The client is establishing the baseline.

What it needs: the full compulsory set, complete interior and exterior, nothing skipped. Take everything, bid from what you find.

Bid request

Estimate to perform work, and any request where the client asks what a repair would cost. You are pricing, not working.

What it needs: the compulsory set, then photos and measurements for everything you bid, including work nobody asked about but that clearly needs doing.

Approval type

Do work per bid, work per approved bid, clear convey per bid, winterization, grass cut and snow removal, maid service, monthly marketability checks, miscellaneous work done. The work is authorized, now you prove it.

What it needs: the compulsory set, clean before, during and after photos of the approved work, plus any additional bids you find while you are there.

Watch this one closely: Do work per bid

Most disagreements on approved work are not about the work at all. They are about what was approved. The client approved one thing, the field team read the approval a different way, and the work that got done does not match the approval. Before you start, open the reference photos attached to the approval, and if they are missing or unclear, ask your processor to pull them. Do the work the approval shows, then photograph it the same way the reference photo is framed so the reviewer can compare them side by side.

7 Before, during, after

One task, three views, every time

This is the single most common reason a work order comes back. Not a missing photo, a missing stage. If you cut grass, show the lot before, a frame of the work happening, and the finished lot. If you board a window, show the opening, the boards going on, and the result. If you cap a water heater, show it uncapped, the cap in hand or being installed, and the cap seated.

BEFORE

Condition as you found it. Wide, then close. This is your protection if damage is blamed on you later.

DURING

The work actually happening. Hand, tool, material in frame. This is what proves the work was done, not just the result.

AFTER

Finished result, framed like the before shot so a reviewer can compare them directly.

Photo slot: before during after on a real task images/blog-safeguard-16-before-during-after-work.jpg A debris pile before, during removal and cleared after, or a broken window before, boarding and boarded. Three frames, same angles.

Seasonal work needs the same discipline

Grass cuts and snow removal get reopened constantly because they feel routine. They are not routine to the client, who is paying per visit and checking dates. Before, during and after of the cut, plus the surrounding shots, plus a correct date stamp, is what turns a routine cut into a paid visit instead of a dispute.

8 Bids: measurements, close ups, justification

The bid should answer why, how and how much without a phone call

A bid that says "repair damaged fence" gives a reviewer nothing to approve. A bid that says repair or replace a damaged fence section, approximately 24 linear feet, posts included, photo 4 through photo 7, at a stated price, is a bid that gets approved. The difference is not writing ability, it is evidence.

  • Measure it and photograph the measurement. Tape or stick in frame with the number readable. Where applicable, one photo of the measurement and one of the whole area it refers to.
  • Close up plus long shot. The close up proves the damage. The long shot proves where it is on the property and how much area it covers.
  • Say why it is needed. Water damage spreading, code violation risk, safety hazard, structural risk. Tie the work to the reason.
  • Say how you will do it. Remove and replace, patch and seal, board and paint. The method justifies the price.
  • Use the right line item. Bluebook integrated cost estimate wording where the client requires it. If line items are not your strength, use a processor. A wrong line item costs a review cycle.
  • Add the bids nobody asked for. On bid requests, and on approvals where you are already on site, photograph and bid anything that clearly needs work. That is how a routine order becomes a bigger one.
  • Use a letterhead where the client wants one, with the bid number, property reference and date. We prepare those as drafts for our clients.

One bid, one clear scope. Bidding "secure broken window" and "board broken window" on the same opening as two lines looks like double dipping even when both happened. Combine them or explain the difference in the wording. Overlapping line items are one of the fastest ways to get a bid flagged for review.

9 Allowables, and when to bid anyway

Most of this work is already priced. Know your numbers before you start

Allowables are the standard amounts a client will pay for a standard task, set per client, per loan type and per region. A lot of everyday preservation work sits on an allowable, which means it can be done without a bid. That is a good thing, it means faster payment, but only if you know what the allowable covers.

Typical tasks that sit on allowables include securing work such as deadbolts, knob locks, padlocks with hasp and rekeys, lockboxes, window locks and slider locks, boarding and reglazing of broken windows, chimney caps, padlocks on garage and outbuilding doors, interior and exterior debris removal, health hazard debris, cleaning a refrigerator, cleaning toilets, capping laundry valves and water heater, installing a dryer vent cover, pressure testing, tree and shrub trimming, and seasonal cuts and snow removal.

When to bid anyway: when the scope is bigger than the allowable covers, when access, height or distance makes the standard price unworkable, when the material required is not what the allowable assumes, or when the condition is worse than the task is priced for. Explain it, photograph it, and let the reviewer decide. What you never do is work past an allowable and hope.

10 The paperwork that carries the money

Photos prove it happened. Paper proves it should be paid

Two documents come up again and again, and both are easy to fix before you submit.

Disposal receipts

When you remove debris, the dump receipt is part of the package. It shows the material left the property and where it went, which is exactly what the client needs if the quantity is ever questioned. If you do not have a receipt format, ask your processor. We keep a draft ready so the receipt is consistent every time.

Photo slot: dump receipt images/blog-safeguard-13-debris-receipt.jpg A disposal receipt photographed next to the empty trailer or the cleared area. Redact any personal details.

Invoices that match the approval

The invoice should match the approved scope line for line, at the approved amount, with the photo references in the notes. When the invoice, the approval and the photos tell the same story, payment does not get held.

11 Reference photos before you start

Five minutes that prevents the most expensive kind of mistake

On any do work per bid or approval order, look at the reference photos attached to the approval before you touch anything. They show what the client actually expects to see when the work is finished. When they are missing, ask your processor to pull them. Doing the wrong repair correctly is still a rejected order, and it is the one mistake that costs both the trip and the payment.

12 One question worth asking

Call before you leave the property, not after

If the work is significant and you are unsure, stop and call your back office before you leave the property. Not after. Standing in the room with the damage in front of you is the only moment when a five minute conversation can still fix the submission. Once you have driven away, a missing angle costs a return trip.

The habit that prevents the most reopens: review the whole package while you are still on site. Photos in order, stages present, dates correct, measurements readable, nothing needed missing. If it is not there yet, take it now.

14 What You Shouldn't Do

Short, and each one costs real money

  • You shouldn't submit without a before photo, even when the task seems routine.
  • You shouldn't photograph a sticker on a table instead of posted where it belongs.
  • You shouldn't work past an allowable without an approved bid.
  • You shouldn't submit an order with the camera date wrong. Portals check dates.
  • You shouldn't label photos after you leave the property.
  • You shouldn't guess what an approval meant. Open the reference photos or ask.
  • You shouldn't do a second repair without bidding it, even if it is obvious.
  • You shouldn't submit debris removal without a disposal receipt.
  • You shouldn't leave the site with a question you could have answered on the phone.

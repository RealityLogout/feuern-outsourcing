Why Safeguard Rejects Bids (and How to Write Ones That Get Approved)

Feuern Outsourcing · Bid & Proposition Writing

Most rejected bids on Safeguard don't get bounced because the price is wrong. They get bounced because the scope doesn't give the reviewer enough to approve it without asking questions — and every question is a delay. After processing thousands of preservation bids, the same handful of mistakes show up again and again. Here's what actually gets a bid rejected, and what to fix.

1. The description doesn't match the photo

If the bid says "repair damaged drywall" and the photo shows a hole the size of a fist next to a water stain covering half the wall, the reviewer sees a mismatch and stops. The scope needs to describe exactly what the photo shows — size, location, and cause — not a generic line that could apply to any property.

Fix: Write the scope from the photo, not from memory. Note the approximate dimensions, the specific location in the room, and what caused the damage if it's visible (water, fire, impact, neglect).

2. No quantities or measurements

"Remove debris from yard" gets questioned. "Remove approximately 15 cubic yards of debris from rear yard, including fallen limbs and loose material" does not. Reviewers approve numbers faster than they approve adjectives.

3. Missing before/during/after photo logic

Certain tasks need a specific photo sequence to be approvable — not just "a photo," but the right photo at the right stage. A bid submitted with only an after photo, when the task requires a documented before condition, gets held even if the work itself was done correctly.

4. Line items that overlap or duplicate

Bidding "secure broken window" and "board broken window" as two separate line items on the same opening is a common way to get a bid flagged for review — even when both tasks genuinely happened. Combine overlapping scope into one clearly worded line, or explicitly separate what's different about each (e.g., different windows).

5. Vocabulary that doesn't match Bluebook-compliant wording

Portals are often reviewed against standardized cost-estimator language. A bid using informal phrasing ("fix the fence") instead of the standard line-item wording ("repair/replace damaged fence section, approx. linear footage") is more likely to need manual correction before it's approved — costing you a review cycle you didn't need to lose.

The pattern underneath all five

Every one of these comes down to the same thing: the bid should let a reviewer approve it without needing to ask a single follow-up question. Specific measurements, matching photos, no overlap, and standard wording aren't extra effort — they're what turns a bid from "pending review" into "approved" on the first pass.
